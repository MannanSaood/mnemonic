    question: A pod's readiness probe starts failing. What does Kubernetes do?
    options:
      - text: Takes the pod out of the Service's endpoints until the probe passes again. Nothing restarts.
        correct: true
        why: Right. Readiness controls traffic only. The container keeps running and gets traffic back when the probe passes.
      - text: Restarts the container.
        why: That is what a failing liveness probe does. Readiness never restarts anything.
      - text: Deletes the pod and creates a new one.
        why: Probes act on containers inside the same pod. Replacing pods is the job of controllers such as a ReplicaSet.
  engineer:
    question: After every kubectl delete, your pod takes exactly 30 seconds to go away, and its logs show no shutdown message. What is the likely cause?
    options:
      - text: The process never handles SIGTERM, so it runs until SIGKILL at the end of the 30 s grace period.
        correct: true
        why: Right. Often it is PID 1 in the container with no handler, which the kernel does not kill on SIGTERM, or a shell entrypoint that does not pass the signal on.
      - text: The liveness probe takes 30 seconds to fail.
        why: Probes stop mattering once the pod is terminating. The 30 s is the default terminationGracePeriodSeconds.
      - text: The kubelet always waits the full grace period before it stops a container.
        why: It sends SIGTERM first. A process that exits on SIGTERM is gone in milliseconds, and the pod with it.
  deep:
    question: The app handles SIGTERM by closing its listener and draining, and has a readiness probe. Clients still see refused connections on every rollout. Why?
    options:
      - text: The pod is removed from the endpoints and sent SIGTERM at the same time, and kube-proxy and load balancers take a few seconds to stop routing to it.
        correct: true
        why: Right. The listener closes while traffic is still arriving. A preStop sleep a little longer than that propagation delay keeps the app serving until nobody sends to it any more.
      - text: The readiness probe has to fail failureThreshold times before the pod leaves the endpoints.
        why: A terminating pod is marked not ready at once, without waiting for any probe. The delay is in propagating that change.
      - text: SIGTERM is delivered to every container in the pod in a random order.
        why: Containers do get SIGTERM in no particular order, but this app is a single container. The refusals come from routing that has not caught up.

  roles:
    - role: Startup probe
      example: /livez, 12 × 5 s
      job: Answers "has it finished starting?". Runs only until the first success; holds liveness and readiness off until then.
    - role: Liveness probe
      example: /livez
      job: Answers "is it beyond repair?". Restarts the container after failureThreshold failures. Must not depend on anything outside the process.
    - role: Readiness probe
      example: /readyz
      job: Answers "should it get traffic now?". Takes the pod out of the endpoints while it fails; may depend on the database.
    - role: preStop hook
      example: "sleep: 5 s"
      job: Runs before SIGTERM. A short sleep keeps the app serving while endpoint removal reaches every load balancer.
    - role: Grace period
      example: terminationGracePeriodSeconds 30
      job: The deadline from the start of shutdown to SIGKILL. preStop and draining must both fit inside it.
  useWhen:
    - Every long-running service behind a Service needs a readiness probe and SIGTERM handling, or it drops requests on each deploy.
    - Add a liveness probe when the process can get stuck in a way only a restart fixes, such as a deadlock or an exhausted event loop.
    - Add a startup probe when start-up is slow or varies, so liveness can stay strict once the app is running.
  avoidWhen:
    - Do not point liveness at a shared dependency. An outage of the dependency then restarts every pod at once, adding load and removing capacity when you can least afford it.
    - Do not use a large initialDelaySeconds on liveness to cover slow starts. It delays detection of real hangs too; a startup probe does the job without that cost.
  tradeoffs:
    - Short probe periods and low thresholds notice problems sooner and also fail on brief slowdowns, such as a garbage collection pause or a slow node.
    - A longer preStop sleep makes shutdowns safer and rollouts slower, and it must stay well inside the grace period.
    - Readiness that checks dependencies protects users from a broken pod, but if every pod checks the same dependency, all of them leave the endpoints together and the Service has nothing to route to.
  lookalikes:
    - name: Load balancer health checks
      how: The same idea one layer out. A cloud load balancer probing nodes or pods acts like readiness, with its own interval and threshold on top of Kubernetes'.
    - name: Process supervisors
      how: systemd's Restart= and watchdog do what liveness does, for one machine. They have no notion of readiness or endpoints.
    - name: Connection draining
      how: Load balancers can keep existing connections open to a deregistered backend for a while. It covers requests in flight, not new requests sent before the change arrives.

## [concept] Probes, startup and graceful shutdown

**The kubelet asks each container three separate questions. Has it finished starting? Is it beyond repair? Should it get traffic now? The <Term id="startup-probe">startup</Term>, <Term id="liveness-probe">liveness</Term> and <Term id="readiness-probe">readiness</Term> probes answer them, and shutdown is a fourth contract: a <Term id="prestop-hook">preStop hook</Term>, SIGTERM, and SIGKILL at a deadline.**

Readiness decides whether traffic comes. Liveness decides whether to restart. Startup holds both off while the app boots. Each must answer only its own question, or a slow start, a database blip or a deploy turns into failed requests.

<Depth level={2}>
Probes can be an HTTP GET (any status from 200 to 399 passes), a TCP connect, a command in the container, or a gRPC health check. Each has initialDelaySeconds, periodSeconds (10 by default), timeoutSeconds (1), and failureThreshold (3). A failing liveness probe restarts the container with growing backoff; a failing readiness probe only takes the pod out of the <Term id="endpoint-slice">EndpointSlice</Term>.
</Depth>

<Depth level={3}>
Shutdown has its own order. The pod is marked terminating, which takes it out of the endpoints and starts the kubelet's shutdown at the same moment. The kubelet runs the preStop hook, then sends SIGTERM to PID 1 of each container, and sends SIGKILL to whatever is left when <Term id="termination-grace-period">terminationGracePeriodSeconds</Term> (30 by default) runs out. If preStop is still running at the deadline, the kubelet allows one extra 2 seconds.
</Depth>

## [problem] One health endpoint for everything

The payments API has a `/health` endpoint that pings the database and returns 200 if it answers. Both the liveness and the readiness probe call it. The process runs the HTTP server and nothing else; it was never written to do anything special on SIGTERM. Deploys happen once a week, at night, and nobody has noticed a problem.

<Depth level={2}>
"Healthy" here means two things at once: this process works, and its database works. Liveness acts on the first; readiness should act on the second. Combined, a database problem looks like a broken process.
</Depth>

<Depth level={3}>
What the process does on SIGTERM depends on the runtime. Go and Node.js exit at once by default. uvicorn drains. A Rust binary installs no handler at all, and as <Term id="pid-one">PID 1 in a container</Term> it ignores the signal until SIGKILL.
</Depth>

## [pain] Then a change request arrives

**Change request:** deploy the payments API ten times a day instead of once a week, without failing a single payment.

The team adds a SIGTERM handler that closes the server. The first day of frequent deploys, the error dashboard shows a burst of refused connections at every rollout, around 80 failed payments each time. A week later the database fails over for 40 seconds. Every pod fails liveness within the same 30 seconds, every pod restarts, and the API is down for two minutes after the database has come back.

<Depth level={2}>
Closing the server on SIGTERM does not help, because SIGTERM arrives while load balancers are still sending to the pod. Endpoint removal and the signal start together, and the removal takes seconds to reach every node. The restart storm is the liveness probe doing exactly what it was told: the database was down, so "healthy" was false.
</Depth>

<Depth level={3}>
Restarts make it worse each time. The kubelet waits 10 s before the second restart, then 20 s, then 40 s, up to 5 minutes: <Term id="crash-loop-backoff">CrashLoopBackOff</Term>. Pods that kept failing liveness during the outage are still waiting out their backoff when the database is back.
</Depth>

## [morph] Give each probe one question

Drag the slider. `/health` splits in two: `/livez` answers only whether the process can respond, and `/readyz` checks the database. SIGTERM handling becomes a drain that starts after a preStop sleep.

Hover a box in the diagram to see its code.

<Depth level={2}>
The pod spec gets a startup probe on `/livez` with a generous budget (failureThreshold × periodSeconds), a liveness probe on `/livez` with the defaults, a readiness probe on `/readyz`, and `lifecycle.preStop` with a 5 second sleep. The `sleep` action runs in the kubelet, so the image needs no sleep binary; it is on by default since Kubernetes 1.30 and stable since 1.34. Older clusters use an exec hook that runs `sleep 5`.
</Depth>

<Depth level={3}>
The app's own drain deadline (20 s here) must leave room inside the grace period for the preStop sleep, or SIGKILL arrives mid-drain. A terminating pod's endpoint stays in the EndpointSlice with `ready: false`, `terminating: true`, and `serving` still true while it can serve, so proxies that understand those conditions can drain more gently.
</Depth>

## [walk] Follow one pod's life

A v2 pod from start, through a database failover, to the next rollout that replaces it. Step through it with the numbered buttons.

<Depth level={3}>
<Lang only="go">`Shutdown` returns `http.ErrServerClosed` from `ListenAndServe` at once, so the goroutine must ignore that error, and `main` must wait for `Shutdown` to return before it exits.</Lang>
<Lang only="python">uvicorn handles SIGTERM itself: it stops accepting, waits for requests in flight up to `--timeout-graceful-shutdown`, then runs the lifespan shutdown after `yield`.</Lang>
<Lang only="typescript">`server.close()` calls back only when every connection has ended, which is why idle keep-alive sockets are closed explicitly and a timer caps the wait.</Lang>
<Lang only="rust">Registering the handler is what makes SIGTERM reach the process at all when it is PID 1. `with_graceful_shutdown` then stops accepting and waits for every connection to finish.</Lang>
</Depth>

## [again] The same week, with honest probes

Ten deploys a day. Each old pod leaves the endpoints, keeps serving for 5 seconds while the load balancers catch up, then drains and exits. No failed payments. When the database fails over, all pods leave the endpoints together and requests fail fast with no healthy backend, but nothing restarts. The moment the database is back, readiness passes and traffic returns in seconds.

<Depth level={2}>
The database outage still costs its 40 seconds; no probe can prevent that. What the probes decide is whether it costs 40 seconds or three minutes.
</Depth>

<Depth level={3}>
Containers in one pod receive SIGTERM in no defined order. If a sidecar proxy exits before the app has drained, the app's last responses are lost. Native sidecar containers (init containers with `restartPolicy: Always`) are stopped after the main containers, which fixes that ordering.
</Depth>

## [lens] How each language does it

Go, Node.js and Rust leave SIGTERM to you, and each gets it wrong in its own way by default: Go and Node exit mid-request, and Rust ignores the signal as PID 1. Python's ASGI servers drain for you. In every language the probe endpoints are a few lines; deciding what each one checks is the real work.

Switch the language at the top of the page; the code, the diagram labels and the note follow.

<Depth level={2}>
Check what actually runs as PID 1. A shell entrypoint that starts the server without `exec`, or a process manager that does not forward signals, swallows SIGTERM whatever the application does. `exec` in the entrypoint, or a small init such as tini, fixes it.
</Depth>
