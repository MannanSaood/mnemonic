# ---------------------------------------------------------------- how it works
walk:
  - text: A customer pays. The middleware counts the response under its status class and puts its latency in a bucket. Nothing is averaged yet.
    nodes: [users, context, ping, avg]
    edges: [users>context, context>ping, context>avg]
    role: count
  - text: Prometheus turns counts into the SLI, good events over valid events across 30 days. A 500 is bad; a 404 is the customer's mistake and still counts as good.
    nodes: [ping, avg, strategy]
    edges: [ping>strategy, avg>strategy]
    role: strategy
  - text: The SLO says 99.9%. The error budget is the other 0.1%, here 72,000 failed checkouts a month, and the rule reports what share of it is left.
    nodes: [strategy, slo, budget]
    edges: [strategy>slo, slo>budget]
    role: budget
  - text: The policy reads the budget. Above half, ship. Below half, slow down. At zero, freeze features until the window has budget again. The release gate runs that rule before every deploy.
    nodes: [budget, policy]
    edges: [budget>policy]
    role: policy

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- checks
checks:
  overview:
    question: An SLO says 99.95% of requests succeed over 30 days. With steady traffic, how long a total outage does the error budget allow?
    options:
      - text: About 22 minutes.
        correct: true
        why: Right. 0.05% of 43,200 minutes is 21.6 minutes. Outages at busy hours cost more, because the budget counts requests.
      - text: About 43 minutes.
        why: That is the budget for 99.9%. Every extra nine divides it by ten; 99.95% halves it.
      - text: About 4 minutes.
        why: That is 99.99%. At 99.95% you may fail 1 request in 2,000.
  engineer:
    question: Checkout's mean latency is 120 ms and has not moved, but customers complain it is slow. Which SLI would have shown the problem?
    options:
      - text: The share of requests answered in under 300 ms.
        correct: true
        why: Right. A threshold SLI counts every slow customer. If 10% of requests take 2 seconds, it drops to 90% even when the mean barely changes.
      - text: The 30-day mean latency with a tighter target.
        why: A mean can stay low while a slow tail grows, because the many fast requests outweigh the few slow ones.
      - text: CPU utilisation of the checkout servers.
        why: Users do not feel CPU. A service can be slow at 20% CPU (waiting on a dependency) and fine at 90%.
  deep:
    question: Your latency SLI uses a Prometheus histogram with the default buckets (… 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1 …) and a 300 ms threshold. What is wrong?
    options:
      - text: No bucket ends at 0.3 s, so the count of requests under 300 ms can only be estimated between the 0.25 and 0.5 buckets.
        correct: true
        why: Right. A histogram only knows counts per bucket. Put a bucket edge exactly on the threshold and count `le="0.3"` directly.
      - text: Histograms cannot measure latency over a 30-day window.
        why: They can; `increase()` of the bucket counters over 30 days works, though long ranges are expensive, so teams precompute shorter windows.
      - text: Histograms record the mean, not individual requests.
        why: They record a count per bucket plus a sum and a count, which is enough for threshold SLIs if the edges are right.

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- reference page
reference:
  roles:
    - role: SLI
      example: non-5xx responses / all requests, measured at the edge
      job: A ratio of good events to valid events, from 0% to 100%, that tracks what users feel.
    - role: SLO
      example: 99.9% over a 30-day rolling window
      job: The target for the SLI. Below it, users are unhappy enough to justify engineering time.
    - role: Error budget
      example: 0.1% of requests, 72,000 failures a month
      job: The failure the SLO allows. Spent on releases, experiments and incidents alike.
    - role: Error budget policy
      example: freeze feature releases at zero, signed by product, dev and SRE
      job: Says in advance what happens as the budget runs low, so nobody negotiates during an outage.
    - role: SLA
      example: a refund below 99.5%
      job: A contract with consequences. Set looser than the SLO so you act before you pay.
  useWhen:
    - Any service with users, internal or external, whose reliability someone argues about.
    - Deciding between reliability work and features with data instead of opinion.
    - Alerting on what users feel instead of on causes like CPU.
  avoidWhen:
    - Setting a target nobody will act on. An SLO without a policy is a dashboard.
    - Choosing SLIs users cannot feel (CPU, memory, uptime of a health check).
    - Aiming for 100%. Users cannot tell 99.999% from 100% through their own networks, and it leaves no room to change anything.
  tradeoffs:
    - Measuring at the server is cheap but misses requests that never reach it; measuring at the load balancer or client sees more and costs more.
    - A rolling window matches what users remember; a calendar window matches planning. The SRE workbook suggests four weeks rolling, so every window has the same weekends.
    - Each extra nine divides the budget by ten and costs far more than the last.
    - Request-based budgets make busy-hour outages expensive and quiet-hour ones cheap. That is usually right, but not for low-traffic services.
  lookalikes:
    - name: Uptime percentage
      how: Measures whether a probe got an answer, not whether customers did. A service can be up and failing half its requests.
    - name: KPI dashboards
      how: Show many numbers with no target and no consequence. An SLO is one number with both.
    - name: Apdex
      how: Also a threshold-based latency score, with a satisfied, tolerating and frustrated band, usually without a budget or policy attached.

## [concept] SLIs and SLOs: measuring what users feel

**A <Term id="sli">service level indicator</Term> is the share of events that went well for users. A <Term id="slo">service level objective</Term> is a target for that share over a window. What the target leaves over is the <Term id="error-budget">error budget</Term>, and a <Term id="error-budget-policy">policy</Term> says what happens when it runs out.**

An SLI is a ratio: good events over valid events, such as checkouts that did not fail, or checkouts answered in under 300 ms. An SLO of 99.9% over 30 days lets 1 checkout in 1,000 fail. Those failures are the budget, and the team may spend it on releases and experiments as well as on accidents.

<Depth level={2}>
Pick few SLIs, close to the user: availability and latency for request/response services, freshness for pipelines, durability for storage. Measure where users meet the service, at the load balancer if you can. Express latency as a <Term id="tail-latency">threshold share</Term>, not a mean. An <Term id="sla">SLA</Term> is different: a contract with refunds, set looser than the SLO so the team reacts before money changes hands.
</Depth>

<Depth level={3}>
Google's SRE books recommend the good-over-valid form because every SLI then looks the same to tooling: a numerator, a denominator and a threshold. The budget is simply 1 minus the SLO, in the same units as the SLI. The window matters: a <Term id="rolling-window">rolling window</Term> of four weeks keeps the number of weekends constant, and a big outage stays in the number for as long as users remember it.
</Depth>

## [problem] A dashboard that is always green

The checkout API has a dashboard. A gauge shows mean latency over the last minute, and an external checker requests `/healthz` once a minute and reports uptime. Both have looked healthy for a year: 120 ms and 99.99%.

<Depth level={2}>
Both numbers measure something real. Neither measures a customer's checkout. `/healthz` answers even when the payment dependency is failing, and the mean of thousands of fast requests barely moves when a few hundred take two seconds.
</Depth>

<Depth level={3}>
A mean over a minute is also lossy in time: averages of averages cannot be combined into a monthly number, and the gauge throws away how many requests each minute had. You cannot rebuild an SLI from it after the fact.
</Depth>

## [pain] Then a change request arrives

**Change request:** product wants a reliability target for checkout, and a rule that says when we stop shipping features to fix reliability.

The marked line is the first attempt: keep the mean under 250 ms and the health check up 99.9% of minutes. That month a dependency makes one checkout in five take 700 ms for eight hours, and a bad release fails every payment for 20 minutes. The mean stays under 250 ms, the health check never fails, and the target is met. Support tickets say otherwise.

<Depth level={2}>
The target measured the wrong things, so it could not say when to stop shipping. Every release meeting turned into an argument between people who saw the tickets and people who saw the dashboard.
</Depth>

<Depth level={3}>
A target over minutes of probe uptime also weighs a 3 a.m. outage the same as one at the afternoon peak. Users do not: four times as many of them hit the afternoon one.
</Depth>

## [morph] Count good events, then budget the bad ones

Drag the slider. The gauge and the probe become a counter by status class and a latency histogram with a bucket edge at the threshold. Recording rules turn them into the SLI and the budget left, and a policy reads the budget.

Hover a box in the diagram to see its code.

<Depth level={2}>
Start the target from what the service does today and what users need, not from a round number. Write it down with its SLI definition, window, exclusions and the reason for each, and get product, development and the on-call team to sign the policy. If one of them will not, the SLO is wrong, not the people.
</Depth>

<Depth level={3}>
`increase()` over 30 days reads a month of samples on every evaluation, which is slow on a busy server. Teams record the ratio over short windows (5 minutes, an hour) and build long windows from those, which also gives the burn-rate alerts in the next lesson their inputs.
</Depth>

## [again] The same month again

The same slow dependency and the same bad release. The latency SLI charges the slow afternoon to the budget, the availability SLI charges the 53,000 failed payments, and by the second bad release the budget is gone. The release gate above then fails the CI step for feature deploys, as the policy says, until the rolling window has budget again.

<Depth level={2}>
The budget also works the other way. A team that ends every month with most of its budget unspent is either shipping too cautiously or holding a target users do not need. Both are worth a conversation.
</Depth>

<Depth level={3}>
The budget is user-facing whoever caused the failure. A dependency's outage spends your budget too; the policy can say whether it triggers a freeze, but the SLI should not hide it.
</Depth>

### Budgets in numbers

| SLO | Bad events allowed | Total outage per 30 days |
|---|---|---|
| 99% | 1 in 100 | 7.2 hours |
| 99.5% | 1 in 200 | 3.6 hours |
| 99.9% | 1 in 1,000 | 43.2 minutes |
| 99.95% | 1 in 2,000 | 21.6 minutes |

Outage minutes assume steady traffic. With a request-based SLI, an outage at peak costs more than its share of the clock, and one at night less.
