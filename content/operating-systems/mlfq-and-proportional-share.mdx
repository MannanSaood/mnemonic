## [concept] MLFQ and proportional share

**A <Term id="mlfq">multi-level feedback queue</Term> learns which jobs are interactive by watching how they use the CPU. Proportional-share schedulers ignore behaviour and give each job a set fraction, by tickets or weights.**

MLFQ approximates shortest-job-first without knowing run times. Proportional share gives you control over who gets how much.

<Depth level={2}>
MLFQ's rules: higher levels run first, and jobs at the same level take turns. New jobs start on top. A job that uses up its allotment at a level moves down one. Every so often a <Term id="priority-boost">priority boost</Term> moves every job back to the top. Proportional share comes in two classic forms. <Term id="lottery-scheduling">Lottery scheduling</Term> draws a random ticket for each slice. <Term id="stride-scheduling">Stride scheduling</Term> runs the job with the lowest pass and advances it by its stride, a big number divided by its tickets.
</Depth>

<Depth level={3}>
Solaris's time-sharing class is a table-driven MLFQ with 60 levels, slices from 20 ms at the top to a few hundred at the bottom, and a boost about once a second. BSD derivatives and Windows also use forms of MLFQ. Linux went the proportional-share way. Its scheduler weights each task by its <Term id="nice-value">nice value</Term> and tracks <Term id="vruntime">virtual runtime</Term>: CPU time used, divided by weight. CFS ran the task with the lowest vruntime from 2007; since Linux 6.6, <Term id="eevdf">EEVDF</Term> keeps the weights and vruntime and picks, among tasks owed CPU, the one with the earliest virtual deadline.
</Depth>

## [problem] Workers that take chunks in arrival order

A SaaS product runs each customer's nightly sync as background jobs. Every job is split into chunks of about 100 ms, and a pool of workers takes chunks from one shared queue in arrival order.

Each tenant submits two or three jobs a night. The queue drains in seconds and nobody waits long.

<Depth level={2}>
The mental model: chunks interleave jobs, so everyone gets a fair turn. That is round robin over chunks, and it is fair per chunk in the queue. A tenant with more chunks queued gets more turns.
</Depth>

<Depth level={3}>
Per-chunk fairness also rewards splitting. A tenant that submits one job as ten small ones gets ten places in the queue. Any policy that counts turns instead of shares can be gamed this way, much as a job can game MLFQ by giving up the CPU at the right moment.
</Depth>

## [pain] Then a change request arrives

**Change request:** onboard an enterprise customer whose nightly sync submits 500 jobs at once.

The marked line is the only difference: the enterprise tenant's loop now submits 500 jobs. That is about 30,000 chunks, six minutes of all eight workers. A team tenant whose sync lands a moment later waits behind every one of them, and its dashboard shows yesterday's data until mid-morning.

<Depth level={2}>
More workers do not change the order. They only shorten the six minutes. What the queue needs is a share per tenant: the enterprise plan pays for more and should get more, but every tenant should make steady progress.
</Depth>

<Depth level={3}>
The cost meter shows the team tenant's wait. Its own work is 3 jobs, 18 seconds of worker time. Nearly all of its turnaround is spent in the queue behind another tenant.
</Depth>

## [morph] From one queue to tickets per tenant

Drag the slider. The single shared queue becomes a runner with one queue per tenant and a pick rule: the tenant with the lowest pass goes next.

The code gives each tenant tickets from its plan and replaces the shared queue with stride scheduling.

<Depth level={2}>
With 8, 4 and 1 tickets, the strides are in the ratio 1 : 2 : 8. When all three have work, every 13 chunks go 8 to enterprise, 4 to team and 1 to free. When a tenant has nothing queued, its share goes to the others, so no worker idles. Lottery would also work: draw a ticket for each chunk. It needs no pass and no care when tenants come and go, but over a short window the shares can drift a long way from the tickets.
</Depth>

<Depth level={3}>
The `max(pass, minPass)` line is the fix stride scheduling always needs. A tenant that was idle for an hour still has the pass it had an hour ago, far below everyone else's. Without the line it would get every chunk until it caught up. Linux's fair scheduler has the same guard: a task that wakes after a sleep is placed near the current minimum vruntime instead of keeping its old one.
</Depth>

### The MLFQ rules, in full

| Rule | What it says | What it prevents |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A higher level runs before a lower one | Batch work delaying interactive work |
| 2 | Jobs at the same level take turns | One job monopolising a level |
| 3 | A new job starts at the top | New short jobs waiting behind old long ones |
| 4 | A job that uses its allotment at a level moves down, however it split its time | Gaming by giving up the CPU early |
| 5 | Every period S, all jobs move to the top | Starvation, and missing jobs that became interactive |

### Nice values as weights

Linux turns a nice value into a weight: 1024 at nice 0, and about 1.25 times more or less for each step. Two CPU-bound tasks at nice 0 and nice 5 split a core roughly 75 : 25 (weights 1024 and 335). A cgroup's `cpu.weight` is converted to the same kind of weight, with the default 100 mapping to 1024, which is how container CPU requests turn into shares.
