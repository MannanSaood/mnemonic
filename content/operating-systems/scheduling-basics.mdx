    - name: Fair queuing

    - name: Load balancing

## [concept] Scheduling basics

**A scheduler picks which ready task runs next. Running the shortest work first gives the best <Term id="turnaround-time">turnaround time</Term>; taking turns gives the best <Term id="response-time">response time</Term>. No policy wins both.**

The kernel's CPU scheduler, a thread pool and your own job queue face the same choice with the same metrics.

<Depth level={2}>
Four classic policies. **FIFO** runs jobs in arrival order, each to completion. **<Term id="shortest-job-first">SJF</Term>** runs the shortest job first, to completion. **<Term id="stcf">STCF</Term>** is preemptive SJF: when a job arrives, whichever has the least time left runs. **<Term id="round-robin">Round robin</Term>** runs each ready job for one <Term id="time-slice">time slice</Term> and moves it to the back. Turnaround is finish time minus arrival; response is first run minus arrival.
</Depth>

<Depth level={3}>
The textbook setup assumes jobs that only compute and whose run times are known. Both assumptions are false in practice. Jobs block on I/O, and an OS treats each CPU burst between I/O waits as a separate short job, so the CPU runs one job while another waits on disk. Run times are unknown, which is why real schedulers estimate them from past behaviour. That is the next lesson.
</Depth>

## [problem] One worker, one queue, arrival order

A web app sends password reset emails and makes thumbnails through a background job queue. One worker per instance pops jobs in arrival order and runs each to completion.

Every job takes well under a second. FIFO is simple, fair in the obvious sense, and nobody has complained.

<Depth level={2}>
The mental model: fair means first come, first served, and the queue is fast because every job is fast. The queue is a scheduler, though, and FIFO only works while the jobs are about the same length.
</Depth>

<Depth level={3}>
FIFO is also non-preemptive. Once a job starts, nothing runs on that worker until it finishes, however long it takes and whatever arrives behind it.
</Depth>

## [pain] Then a change request arrives

**Change request:** the monthly invoice export for each customer, about two minutes per job, now runs on the same job queue.

The marked lines add it. On the first of the month, users ask for password resets and the emails arrive minutes later, after the reset links have expired. The workers are busy, but nothing is broken and no job is slow on its own. It is the <Term id="convoy-effect">convoy effect</Term>: every short job behind an export waits for all of it.

<Depth level={2}>
The queue-wait histogram shows the shape: most jobs start at once, and a tail matches the export length almost exactly. Adding workers moves the tail but keeps it, because each worker can still pick up an export ahead of a burst of emails.
</Depth>

<Depth level={3}>
The cost meter shows the worst wait of a reset email. Its own run time is 50 ms. Nearly all of its turnaround is spent behind the export.
</Depth>

## [morph] From one FIFO queue to lanes and slices

Drag the slider. The single FIFO queue becomes a worker with a pick rule: a short lane served first, and a long lane whose jobs run one chunk at a time.

The code adds the two lanes and splits the export into chunks that re-queue themselves.

<Depth level={2}>
Two ideas from the classic policies, adapted to a queue where exact run times are unknown. The short lane approximates SJF by job kind. Chunking the export makes it preemptible, so the worker can take a turn at the short lane between chunks, as round robin does.
</Depth>

<Depth level={3}>
The chunk size is the time slice. Ten-row chunks would re-queue thousands of times, each with a round trip to the queue. Ten-minute chunks would bring the convoy back. About a second keeps the overhead to a few percent and the email wait to about a second.
</Depth>

## [walk] Follow one email past an export

One reset email and one export through the lanes. Step through it with the numbered buttons.

<Depth level={3}>
The kernel faces the same problem with no lanes declared for it. Linux's fair scheduler gives each runnable thread a share of the CPU and tracks how much each has used against its share. A thread that mostly sleeps, waiting on I/O, has used little, so when it wakes it is usually picked soon. The kernel learns from behaviour what this worker had to be told.
</Depth>

## [again] Export night, the second time

The same exports, the same emails. A reset email now waits at most one chunk, about a second, and the exports take a few percent longer in total for the extra trips through the queue.

<Depth level={2}>
Measure both metrics. Queue wait per job kind is the response time; enqueue to done for an export is its turnaround. A policy change that improves one usually costs the other, and you want to see both numbers move.
</Depth>

<Depth level={3}>
Shortest-first has a failure of its own: if short jobs never stop arriving, the long lane never runs. A worker that polls the short lane first needs a guard, such as taking a long-lane job every N short ones or after a maximum wait.
</Depth>

## [lens] How each runtime orders its own work

Every runtime has a scheduler of its own between your code and the kernel. Go's is close to round robin with a 10 ms slice. asyncio's and Node's are FIFO without preemption. Tokio's is FIFO with a budget.
