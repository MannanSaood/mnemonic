        correct: true
        why: Right. Queueing delay grows roughly like ρ / (1 − ρ). The work per job is the same; the time spent waiting for a free worker is what explodes.
      - text: Each job takes longer to process when the workers are busier.
        why: The service time is the same. The extra latency is time spent in the queue before the work starts.
      - text: It does not; 90% busy still leaves 10% spare capacity, so latency stays the same.
        why: Spare capacity on average is not spare capacity at every moment. Random arrivals bunch up, and near full there is no slack to absorb the bunches.
  engineer:
    question: A service handles 500 requests a second with an average latency of 200 ms. How many requests are in flight at any moment, on average?
    options:
      - text: "100"
        correct: true
        why: Right. Little's law, L = λW = 500 × 0.2. It holds for any stable system, whatever the distributions, and it is how you size pools and connection limits.
      - text: "2,500"
        why: That divides 500 by 0.2. In-flight work is rate times time, not rate divided by time.
      - text: It depends on the latency distribution, so it cannot be computed.
        why: Little's law needs only the averages. The distribution matters for percentiles, not for the mean number in flight.
  deep:
    question: A dashboard shows the database host at 40% CPU, averaged over 5 minutes, yet users see stalls of a second or more. What is the most likely explanation?
    options:
      - text: Load arrives in bursts. For a few seconds at a time the CPU is saturated and requests queue; the 5-minute average spreads those seconds over quiet ones.
        correct: true
        why: Right. Utilisation is an average over an interval, and queues form at the scale of milliseconds. Per-second sampling, queue lengths or scheduler latency show the bursts.

        why: Throttling would show up as a cap in the metrics, and it would slow every request, not cause occasional stalls on an idle-looking host.

      example: 95 checkouts a second
      job: How fast work arrives. Plan for the peak, and for bursts within the peak.
    - role: Service time (S)
      example: 40 ms of CPU per checkout
      job: How long one job holds a worker, excluding waiting. Measure it; do not assume it.
    - role: Utilisation (ρ = λS / c)
      example: 95 × 0.04 / 6 = 63%
      job: The share of time workers are busy. Where on the curve you are.
    - role: Queue
      example: { go: "chan Checkout, 12", python: "asyncio.Semaphore(18)", typescript: "Piscina maxQueue", rust: "crossbeam bounded(12)" }
      job: Absorbs bursts. Its length is the saturation signal; its bound decides between a fast rejection and a slow timeout.
  useWhen:
    - Sizing any pool, connection limit or partition count. Little's law gives the busy count; headroom gives the rest.
    - Deciding capacity before a known peak. Aim for utilisation on the flat part of the curve, often 60 to 75% for latency-sensitive work.
    - Reading a dashboard. Look for queue lengths and wait times next to utilisation.
  avoidWhen:
    - Unbounded queues in request paths. They turn overload into memory growth and timeouts instead of a clear signal.
    - Planning from 5-minute averages when work arrives in bursts.
  tradeoffs:
    - Headroom costs money; the lack of it costs latency. The curve says how much latency each point of utilisation buys.
    - A short queue sheds load early and keeps latency bounded, but rejects work that a longer queue would have served late.
    - More, smaller workers sharing one queue beat the same capacity split into separate queues; a free worker never sits idle while another has a line.
    - Less variable service times halve waiting at the same utilisation. Splitting slow and fast jobs into separate pools helps both.
  lookalikes:
    - name: Backpressure
      how: A full queue slowing the producer down, instead of rejecting. Works inside a pipeline; at a public API, clients just see a slow response.
    - name: Load shedding
      how: Rejecting early when saturated. The bounded queue here is the simplest form.
    - name: Autoscaling
      how: Adds workers when utilisation rises. It reacts in minutes, so it cannot absorb bursts measured in seconds.

## [concept] Queues and the utilisation cliff

**When work arrives at random, the time it waits for a busy resource grows like ρ / (1 − ρ), where ρ is the resource's <Term id="utilisation">utilisation</Term>. Waiting stays small for a long time, then explodes as the resource nears 100%.**

Every resource with a queue in front of it behaves this way: worker pools, connection pools, disks, locks, CPUs. Fast at 60% busy can mean very slow at 95%, with no change in the code.

<Depth level={2}>
<Term id="saturation">Saturation</Term> is the work waiting, the length of the queue. <Term id="queueing-delay">Queueing delay</Term> is the time it waits. <Term id="littles-law">Little's law</Term> ties the averages together: items in the system = arrival rate × time each spends inside. Use it both ways. 500 requests a second at 200 ms each means 100 in flight. 95 jobs a second at 40 ms of work each keeps 3.8 workers busy.
</Depth>

<Depth level={3}>
The ρ / (1 − ρ) shape is exact for the simplest queue, one server with random (Poisson) arrivals and exponentially distributed service times, where the mean wait is S × ρ / (1 − ρ). With c servers sharing one queue, the Erlang C formula gives the chance a job waits at all. Variability scales the curve: constant service times halve the wait at the same utilisation, burstier arrivals raise it. The cliff stays.
</Depth>

## [problem] A fraud-check pool sized for the average

A payments service scores every checkout with a fraud model before it authorises the card. Scoring takes about 40 ms of CPU, so a pool of 4 workers can score 100 checkouts a second. Last year's peak was 80. A startup check makes sure the peak stays under capacity, and the queue in front of the workers is large enough never to fill.

Read it with the usual mental model: 80 a second is 80% of capacity, so there is 20% to spare, and latency stays at 40 ms until the pool is full.

<Depth level={2}>
At 80% busy the pool already queues. Checkouts arrive at random, so some seconds bring 90 and some 70, and within a second they bunch. With 4 workers at 80%, 6 checkouts in 10 wait for a worker, about 30 ms on average and about 200 ms at the 99th percentile.
</Depth>

<Depth level={3}>
The large queue looks defensive. It hides the problem instead: when the pool falls behind, work piles up in memory and every checkout waits longer, until callers time out. The only visible signal is latency, and by then the queue is minutes long.
</Depth>

## [pain] Then a change request arrives

**Change request:** a flash sale is coming. Plan the fraud-check pool for 95 checkouts a second at peak, up from 80.

The marked line is the only change, and the startup check still passes: 95 is under 100. During the sale, checkout latency jumps. The mean wait for a worker goes from about 30 ms to about 180 ms, the p99 wait from about 200 ms to about 900 ms, and the payment gateway's own timeouts start to fire. Traffic rose 19%. Waiting rose 6 times.

<Depth level={2}>
The pool went from 80% to 95% busy, from the bend of the curve to the cliff. Each point of utilisation near 100% costs more waiting than the one before. Any burst during the sale, or a slower score for one kind of basket, pushes it past 100% for a while, and the queue then grows until the burst ends.
</Depth>

<Depth level={3}>
These numbers come from the M/M/c model with 4 servers (Erlang C). Real arrivals are often burstier than Poisson during a sale, which makes it worse. The cost meter shows the mean wait at the forecast peak.
</Depth>

## [morph] Size it from Little's law, and bound the queue

Drag the slider. The plan that compares the peak with capacity becomes a pool sized for a target utilisation, a short queue that rejects early, and a gauge on its depth.

Hover a box in the diagram to see its code.

<Depth level={2}>
Little's law gives the busy workers: 95 × 0.04 = 3.8. Dividing by a 70% target gives 6 workers, each 63% busy, and a mean wait of about 4 ms. The short queue absorbs normal bunching; anything beyond it is <Term id="load-shedding">shed</Term> with a fast 503, so overload shows up as a count of rejections, not as timeouts everywhere.
</Depth>

<Depth level={3}>
Where the work comes from a producer you control, such as a stream consumer, a full queue can block the producer instead, which is <Term id="backpressure">backpressure</Term>. At a public API there is no producer to slow down, only clients, so rejecting is the honest answer.
</Depth>

## [walk] Follow one checkout

<Depth level={3}>
<Lang only="go">`select` with a `default` case tries the send and falls through at once if the buffer is full. The gauge reads `len(jobs)` only when Prometheus scrapes, so it costs nothing per checkout.</Lang>
<Lang only="python">`Semaphore.locked()` is true when no permits are left, so the check fails fast instead of queueing on the semaphore itself. The process pool still has its own internal queue; the semaphore keeps it short.</Lang>
<Lang only="typescript">Piscina rejects `run()` once `maxQueue` tasks are waiting. The gauge's `collect` runs at scrape time and reads `queueSize`.</Lang>

</Depth>

## [again] The flash sale, the second time

The same 95 checkouts a second on 6 workers sized from Little's law: each worker is 63% busy, checkouts wait about 4 ms on average and about 60 ms at the 99th percentile, and a burst that fills the short queue gets instant 503s that the client retries. The queue depth gauge shows the burst as it happens.

<Depth level={2}>
Alert on queue depth or wait time, not only on CPU. Utilisation tells you where you are on the curve; saturation tells you that you have gone over the edge.
</Depth>

<Depth level={3}>
Re-measure the service time after every model change. Little's law is only as good as S: if a new fraud model takes 60 ms, the same 6 workers are 95% busy again.
</Depth>

### The curve in numbers

Mean wait for one worker with random arrivals and random job times (M/M/1), in multiples of the service time:

| Utilisation | 50% | 70% | 80% | 90% | 95% | 99% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mean wait ÷ service time | 1 | 2.3 | 4 | 9 | 19 | 99 |

Several workers sharing one queue sit lower on the same shape, and constant job times halve every number. Past 100% there is no number: the queue grows for as long as the overload lasts.
